## Introduction · *Band 1 — Foundation*

Along many of the world's coastlines, where harbour walls meet the sea, you can find heaps of strange
concrete shapes piled against the waves. Each looks a little like a giant jack from a children's game — two
angled arms joined by a central bar. They are called **dolosse** (a single one is a **dolos**), and they
were invented in **South Africa**, in the harbour city of **East London**, in the early 1960s.

A dolos has one job: to break the force of the sea. Ocean waves striking a harbour can tear apart even
enormous stone walls. Dolosse are laid in loose, interlocking heaps in front of those walls, and instead of
standing against the waves like a solid barrier, they **let the water through and scatter its energy** among
thousands of gaps. It is a small, clever idea that quietly protects harbours on every continent — and it
began on the Eastern Cape coast.

This record tells what a dolos is, how it works, where it came from, and — just as importantly — where it
was *pushed too far*. An honest account of an invention includes its limits, not only its triumph.

*Supported by:* Encyclopædia Britannica — "dolos"; South African Institution of Civil Engineering — accounts
of the dolos.

## The dolos's world · *Band 2 — Context*

The dolos was born from a very practical problem. **East London** is South Africa's only river port, on the
**Buffalo River**, and its harbour walls — **breakwaters** — took a constant beating from the Indian Ocean.
By the early 1960s the blocks then used to armour those walls were being dislodged and broken by storms.

This was the world of **coastal engineering** — the branch of engineering that defends shorelines and
harbours from the sea. Engineers already had "armour units": specially shaped concrete blocks laid over a
breakwater's surface to absorb wave energy. The best-known was the **tetrapod**, a four-legged unit invented
in France in 1950. The dolos belongs to that family — it was the next idea in a running international effort
to find a shape the sea could not defeat. What made the dolos notable is that this contribution came not from
a great imperial laboratory but from a working harbour on the South African coast.

*Supported by:* South African Institution of Civil Engineering — accounts of the dolos; general coastal
engineering references on armour units.

## What a dolos is, and how it works · *Band 1 — Foundation (the account)*

A dolos is an **unreinforced concrete block** in a deliberate, three-part shape: two flukes (angled arms)
set at right angles, joined by a central shank. That awkward-looking geometry is the whole point. When many
dolosse are tipped into place, they **interlock** — hooking loosely into one another — and they leave large
gaps between them.

Those two properties, **interlock** and **permeability**, are how a dolos beats the sea. A solid wall meets a
wave head-on and takes its full force; eventually the wave wins. A heap of dolosse does the opposite: the
wave rushes *into* the gaps, breaks up, and loses its energy in turbulence and friction among the blocks,
while the interlocking arms stop individual units from being plucked out and thrown away. The structure
survives by **absorbing** the sea rather than **resisting** it.

Dolosse range from a few tonnes to very large — some of the biggest exceed forty tonnes each — and a single
breakwater may be armoured with thousands of them. Crucially, the design was **never patented**. Its makers
did not lock it away; it spread freely, and today dolosse (and shapes derived from them) protect coastlines
around the world. An idea given away, rather than owned, is part of what made it global.

*Supported by:* Encyclopædia Britannica — "dolos"; Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (South
Africa) — hydraulic model studies of dolos armour.

## Where it came from — and a contested credit · *Band 1 — Foundation (the account)*

The dolos appeared at East London harbour around **1963–64**, and here the honest record must slow down,
because *who invented it* is genuinely disputed.

Two names are attached to it. **Eric Merrifield**, the harbour engineer in charge at East London, championed
the dolos, put it into service, and did much to make it known to the engineering world. **Aubrey Kruger**, a
draughtsman working there, is credited by many accounts with conceiving the actual shape. For years the
public story favoured the senior engineer; later accounts have worked to restore the technician's role. The
truthful position is not to crown one inventor but to report the dispute: **the dolos came out of a working
harbour team, and the credit between the engineer who advanced it and the draughtsman who is said to have
designed it is contested.** That, too, is a lesson — about how histories of invention often flatten a group's
work into a single famous name.

The name itself carries South African culture. A **dolos** is a knucklebone — the small anklebones of sheep
or goats that traditional diviners cast to "throw the bones." The concrete unit was named for the shape it
resembles. A global piece of engineering thus carries, in its name, a thread of Southern African tradition.

*Supported by:* South African Institution of Civil Engineering — accounts of the dolos and its attribution.

## When it was pushed too far · *Band 1 — Foundation (the account)*

An invention's honest story includes its failures, and the dolos has an important one.

Because dolosse worked so well, engineers built them **bigger** — and bigger dolosse are not simply small
ones scaled up. A dolos is unreinforced concrete, with slender arms, and as the blocks grew, the stresses
inside them — from their own weight and from the pounding of waves — grew faster than the concrete's strength.
Large dolosse could **crack and break**, and a broken armour layer stops protecting anything.

This lesson was driven home at the breakwater of **Sines, in Portugal**, which suffered a major failure in
**1978**, with great numbers of large dolosse breaking apart in heavy seas. The disaster prompted serious
international research into the **structural strength** of armour units, not just their shape and weight —
including model studies in South Africa at the **CSIR**. The dolos was not "wrong"; it had simply been taken
past a limit no one had yet mapped. Engineering advanced by studying the failure, which is how engineering
usually advances.

*Supported by:* Coastal engineering literature on the Sines breakwater failure (1978); CSIR (South Africa) —
studies of dolos structural integrity.

## Why the dolos matters · *Band 1 — Foundation (why it matters)*

Three reasons worth carrying away.

**It is a South African idea that quietly protects the world.** Most people who walk past a breakwater never
learn that those strange concrete shapes were devised on the Eastern Cape coast. The dolos is a reminder that
significant invention does not only come from famous laboratories in wealthy countries.

**It teaches how good engineering actually thinks.** The dolos does not fight the sea; it *absorbs* it — win
by yielding cleverly, not by brute strength. And when scaled too far it failed, and engineers learned from
the failure. Both halves are the real shape of progress.

**It is an idea that was given away.** Unpatented, it spread freely to every continent. In an age that
instinctively asks who *owns* an idea, the dolos is a quiet argument for what happens when a good one is
simply shared.

## Misconceptions and clarifications · *Band 1 — Foundation (required)*

**Misconception:** *"A dolos is just a randomly shaped lump of concrete."*
**Clarification:** The shape is deliberate and functional. Its interlocking arms and the large gaps between
units let waves break up and lose energy *inside* the heap, while the interlock stops blocks being torn out —
a solid wall cannot do either. *(Encyclopædia Britannica — "dolos".)*

**Misconception:** *"One genius inventor designed the dolos."*
**Clarification:** The credit is genuinely disputed. It came from a harbour engineering team at East London;
Eric Merrifield advanced and publicised it, while Aubrey Kruger is credited by many accounts with the actual
design. An honest record reports the dispute rather than crowning one name. *(SAICE — accounts of the dolos.)*

**Misconception:** *"Bigger armour blocks are always stronger and safer."*
**Clarification:** No. Because dolosse are unreinforced concrete, scaling them up increases internal stresses
faster than strength, so very large ones can crack — a lesson driven home by the 1978 Sines breakwater
failure, which prompted research into structural limits, not just shape. *(Coastal engineering literature on
Sines, 1978.)*

## Glossary · *Band 4 — Exploration*

- **Dolos** (pl. *dolosse*) — an interlocking concrete armour unit, invented in South Africa, used to protect
  breakwaters; also the Southern African word for the knucklebones used in divination.
- **Breakwater** — a barrier built to protect a harbour or shore from the force of waves.
- **Armour unit** — a shaped block (dolos, tetrapod, and others) laid over a breakwater to absorb wave energy.
- **Interlock** — the way armour units hook loosely into one another so the sea cannot pluck them out singly.
- **Permeability** — the gaps in a heap of units that let water pass through and lose its energy.
- **Tetrapod** — an earlier four-legged concrete armour unit, invented in France in 1950.
- **Unreinforced concrete** — concrete with no internal steel; strong in compression but able to crack under
  bending or tension — central to why very large dolosse could fail.
- **CSIR** — South Africa's Council for Scientific and Industrial Research.

## Editorial verification review — ⚠️ *author self-review, not independent*

A passage-by-passage review was run (2026-07-26). It **corrected four passages where the wording ran ahead of
the evidence**:

1. The invention date was first written as a flat "1963"; softened to *"around 1963–64"* — sources vary on the
   exact year and it should be shown as approximate.
2. Inventor credit was first written favouring Merrifield; changed to *report the dispute* between Merrifield
   and Kruger rather than settle it. This is the record's key honesty point and must not pick a winner.
3. Dolos size was first given as a specific maximum ("up to 80 tonnes"); softened to *"some of the biggest
   exceed forty tonnes"* — the exact upper figure is uncertain and an unverified precise number is worse than
   an honest floor.
4. The Sines failure was first written as caused *solely* by dolos size; qualified to size being a major
   factor that *prompted* structural research — the full engineering post-mortem is more complex than one
   sentence, and the record should not overstate a single cause.

**The boundary this review does not cross:** it was done by the record's **author**. It catches overstatement;
it does **not** replace an independent coastal-engineer's/historian's review or a teacher's review. Both remain
open. Specific technical figures (dolos weights, the exact Sines sequence, invention year) are flagged for
source verification.
